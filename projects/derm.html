Dermoscopic Lesion Segmentation.

Deep learning approach to dermoscopic skin-lesion segmentation using a U-Net convolutional architecture. Master's thesis ranked 1st in cohort. Demonstrates depth in deep learning, image segmentation, and PyTorch.

The problem

Dermoscopic skin-lesion segmentation is a foundational problem in computer-aided dermatology: accurately delineating lesion boundaries from healthy skin in dermoscopic images, as a precursor to automated malignancy classification.

The challenge is the combination of high inter-class variability (lesions vary enormously in colour, texture, shape, and size) with low contrast at boundaries (the transition from lesion to skin is often gradual rather than sharp). Classical computer vision techniques struggle here — the boundary detection problem is not analytically solvable, and the variation across the dataset means hand-engineered features generalize poorly.

The thesis question: can a deep learning model produce accurate pixel-level segmentation masks across the full range of clinical variation in standard dermoscopic datasets?

Approach

Architecture: U-Net

I used a CNN-based encoder–decoder architecture with skip connections from the U-Net family. The reasoning:

  • The encoder compresses spatial information while building up semantic features (what is this region?).
  • The decoder upsamples back to the original resolution while reconstructing spatial detail (where exactly is the boundary?).
  • Skip connections from encoder to decoder pass spatial detail directly across, letting the model use both the high-level semantic context and the fine-grained pixel detail simultaneously.

For boundary-sensitive tasks like lesion segmentation, U-Net is the natural choice because it preserves spatial precision in a way that pure encoder-decoder networks cannot.

Dataset and augmentation

Trained on the ISIC dataset — the standard benchmark for skin-lesion analysis. Applied extensive data augmentation to handle the high natural variation in lesion appearance:

  • Random rotations and flips (lesions have no canonical orientation).
  • Colour jitter (handle variation in lighting and skin tone).
  • Elastic deformations (simulate the natural variation in lesion shape).
  • Random crops and rescaling (handle variation in capture distance).

Augmentation effectively multiplied the training set size and meaningfully improved generalization to unseen lesion types.

Loss function

Combined two losses:

  • Dice loss for spatial accuracy — directly optimizes the metric we care about (overlap between predicted and true mask). Robust to class imbalance.
  • Binary cross-entropy for pixel-level confidence — provides smoother gradients early in training.

The combination gave more stable training than either alone — Dice handles the imbalance between lesion and skin pixels, BCE keeps gradients flowing through the early epochs before the Dice signal becomes informative.

Evaluation

Standard segmentation metrics on a held-out validation split:

  • Dice coefficient — overlap measure (harmonic mean of precision and recall on pixels).
  • Jaccard index (IoU) — intersection over union, the strict version.
  • Sensitivity — proportion of lesion pixels correctly identified.
  • Specificity — proportion of skin pixels correctly classified.

Compared against published baselines from the ISIC challenge. The model reached competitive performance for the architecture class, with the augmentation and combined loss strategy contributing meaningfully to the final scores.

Outcome

Thesis ranked 1st in cohort. Demonstrated end-to-end deep learning capability — from data preparation through architecture choice, training strategy, evaluation, and academic writing.

Even when the day-to-day work is tabular ML and pipelines, the deep learning foundation matters — it's what gives you the vocabulary to evaluate when DL is the right tool and when it's overkill.

While my professional work since has focused on tabular ML and analytics engineering, the deep learning foundation from this thesis stays relevant: it shapes how I evaluate when DL is the right tool versus when simpler models will do, and it gives me the background to engage credibly with computer vision and deep learning teams when those problems come up at work.
