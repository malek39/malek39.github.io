Behavioural OKR Framework.

Bottom-up KPI decomposition framework for Heetch's Growth org. Breaks the 3 company KPIs (rides, GMV, revenue) down through marketplace × requester × driver segments to behavioural contribution per user per day. Connects Paid Acquisition, Activation, and CRM teams to segment-level targets, with CAC × LTV budget constraints making the system financially closed-loop. Owned end-to-end as primary data partner to the VP of Growth.

The problem

Growth orgs typically set top-down OKRs: "we need X rides this quarter." Then three teams — Paid Acquisition, Activation, CRM — each get a piece of the goal and try to deliver. The problem is that the link between team activity and the top-line number is opaque. Did CRM hit its sub-target because reactivation worked, or because Paid Acquisition brought in a high-quality cohort that month? Why did the company miss the rides target despite every team hitting its own KR?

The root cause is that top-down OKRs aren't grounded in the actual behavioural mechanics of the marketplace. A ride happens because:

  • A passenger of some behavioural segment requests a ride at their characteristic frequency.
  • A driver of some segment is active at that moment to serve the request.
  • The match is successful — supply and demand met at an acceptable price.

If you don't model these mechanics explicitly, you can't tell which lever to pull. The framework I built reverses the direction: start from behaviour at the segment level and roll up to the company KPI. That makes every number directly actionable by a specific team.

The framework: bottom-up KPI decomposition

Step 1 — Anchor in behavioural contribution

For every passenger and driver segment from the segmentation pipeline, compute the behavioural contribution per user per day:

# Passenger behaviour: rides per user per day Hero passenger → 1 ride every 10 days = 0.10 rides/user/day Frequent passenger → 1 ride every 20 days = 0.05 rides/user/day Occasional → 1 ride every 45 days = 0.022 rides/user/day Rare → 1 ride every 120 days = 0.008 rides/user/day # Driver behaviour: rides served per active driver per day Power driver = 12 rides/driver/day Regular driver = 6 rides/driver/day Casual driver = 2 rides/driver/day

These contribution rates are measured, not assumed — pulled directly from the segmentation pipeline rolling-window averages. They're refreshed weekly so the framework adapts as behaviour shifts.

Step 2 — Build the demand equation

Working backwards from the rides target:

# Want: 700,000 rides this week (= 100k/day) target_rides_per_day = 100_000 # Decompose by passenger segment, given their behavioural contribution: required_active_passengers = sum( target_rides_per_day_in_segment_i / contribution_rate_i for segment_i in passenger_segments ) # For one segment (Hero): if 30% of demand comes from Heroes contributing 0.10 rides/day: hero_demand = 100_000 * 0.30 = 30_000 rides/day hero_active_users_needed = 30_000 / 0.10 = 300_000 active Heroes

Repeat per segment. The result is a concrete target population per segment — exactly the kind of target that CRM and Activation can be held accountable for.

Step 3 — Build the supply equation

Mirror the same logic on the driver side:

# How many active drivers do we need to serve 100k rides/day? required_active_drivers = sum( target_rides_per_day_in_segment_j / driver_contribution_rate_j for segment_j in driver_segments ) # If 50% of supply comes from Power drivers contributing 12 rides/day: power_supply_target = 100_000 * 0.50 = 50_000 rides/day power_drivers_needed = 50_000 / 12 = ~4_167 active Power drivers

Step 4 — Allocate to teams by segment

Each team owns the segments they can influence. This is where the framework stops being just an equation and becomes an organisational tool:

  • Paid Acquisition owns: new user inflow into each segment — from acquisition channels (Meta, Google, Adjust, Apple Search) at the entry-point behavioural class.
  • Activation owns: conversion of new users into active behavioural segments — first ride within N days, second ride retention, lifecycle-stage progression.
  • CRM owns: reactivation of dormant users and retention of active segments — keeping Heroes as Heroes, pulling At-Risk back into Active.

For each team, the framework produces a concrete weekly target per segment: "Acquire 12,000 new mid-tier passengers this week. Activate 4,800 of them to first-ride within 14 days. Retain 95% of the existing Hero base."

The flow

Bottom-up flow — from segment behaviour to team OKRs

Closing the loop with CAC × LTV constraints

Segment targets alone aren't enough — they could be financially impossible. The framework closes the loop by attaching a budget envelope to every team and segment combination, governed by CAC and LTV (from the Bayesian CLV model):

# For each team × segment, the financial guard rails: new_users_acquired_in_segment * CAC_segment ≤ acquisition_budget_segment LTV_segment ≥ CAC_segment * payback_threshold # Each team gets: # - a volume target per segment (from the behavioural equation) # - a max CAC per segment (from the LTV constraint) # - a budget envelope (volume × max CAC)

The CLV model feeds the LTV side; historical campaign data feeds the CAC side. The framework refuses to recommend a segment-target combination that violates the payback constraint — which prevents the classic Growth failure mode of "we hit our acquisition target but blew the budget on segments that will never pay back."

Pipeline & operationalisation

  • Inputs. Segmentation pipeline (passenger and driver segments + behavioural contribution rates), CLV model (LTV per segment), historical campaign data (CAC per channel × segment), top-down company target.
  • Engine. BigQuery + Dataform — bottom-up equation runs daily, refreshing target populations and budget envelopes per team × segment.
  • Output. Looker dashboard the VP of Growth uses in weekly reviews: target attainment per team × segment, budget burn vs envelope, projected trajectory vs end-of-quarter target.
  • Adaptive. Because contribution rates are measured (not assumed), the framework self-adjusts as behaviour shifts. If Hero passengers start riding more frequently in summer, the target population for Heroes auto-decreases for the same ride goal.
The framework turned "we need more rides" into "Paid Acquisition needs 12,000 new mid-tier passengers, Activation needs to convert 4,800 to first-ride, CRM needs to keep our 280,000 active Heroes within their 0.10/day contribution — and here's the budget envelope for each."

Outcome

Replaced top-down OKR setting with bottom-up behavioural decomposition. Teams now have concrete, segment-level weekly targets that connect mechanically to the company KPIs — and budget envelopes that prevent unprofitable growth.

Made it possible to detect drift early in the quarter: if Hero retention is slipping, the framework surfaces it in the segment view before it shows up in the top-line rides number. CRM can intervene while there's still time to course-correct.

Adopted by the VP of Growth as the standard planning framework for quarterly OKR setting and weekly Growth reviews.
